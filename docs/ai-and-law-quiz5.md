## Main points

- Compare a model with the actual human decision process. Auditability can help
  identify bias, while scale can spread it. Examine past decisions, collection,
  feedback loops, and proxies.
- Fairness metrics can conflict. The deck contrasts predictive parity with
  false-positive balance; choosing a governing standard requires judgment.
- Exposure is different from displacement, projections from observations, and
  announced cuts from established causal effects. Net job gains can hide churn.
  Novice productivity gains and fewer entry-level openings can coexist.
- Policy choices include tax incentives, upskilling, education, and worker voice.
  Notice, severance, retraining, and access to new roles are examples for the
  discussion of obligations beyond the legal minimum.
- Florida makes each signer responsible for the existence and citation accuracy
  of authorities. AI-assisted drafting does not transfer professional judgment.
- The assigned religious and governance readings both call for human involvement.
  The deck distinguishes their accounts of human limits and the purpose of
  oversight. Overreliance, apparent objectivity, simulated relationships, and
  deskilling are central risks.

## Scope and source checks

The seminar deliberately leaves moral triage, who should choose a fairness
standard, obligations beyond law, and differences in kind versus degree open.
Drills recall the analytical concepts and the readings' positions; they do not
grade a preferred moral conclusion. Claire, HumaTech, and Aperture are fictional.
Classroom activities are not instructions to contact anyone or submit work.

Exact labor-market percentages, dated forecast totals, hypothetical headcounts,
classroom timing, and detailed multi-state statutes are not required recall.
The slide 8 notes caution that the updated employment evidence is descriptive
and sensitive to sample and controls. The quiz tests that limitation rather
than asserting the dated numerical updates as independently established facts.

Main-point authorities checked November 2, 2026:

- [Silberg & Manyika, McKinsey (2019)](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/artificial-intelligence/tackling-bias-in-artificial-intelligence-and-in-humans).
- [Stropoli, Chicago Booth Review (2023)](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/ai-is-going-disrupt-labor-market-it-doesnt-have-destroy-it).
- [Florida Supreme Court, SC2026-0673](https://flcourts-media.flcourts.gov/content/download/2489374/opinion/Opinion_SC2026-0673.pdf),
  amending Rule 2.515(d)(2), including (D), effective June 15, 2026.
- [AOSC26-12](https://flcourts-media.flcourts.gov/content/download/2489379/29182858),
  barring local AI-use disclosure and accuracy-certification requirements, while
  preserving education, warnings, and internal court-employee policies.
- [Magnifica Humanitas](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html),
  assigned paragraphs 100–101 and 118–121; signed May 15, 2026.

Clarifications prevent shorthand from becoming categorical rules: a proxy does
not resolve every discrimination claim; routing an audit through counsel does
not automatically establish privilege; and the actual-human comparison is a
practical baseline rather than a complete disparate-impact test. The deck's
Colorado timing and Illinois liability shorthand are not tested. Predictive
parity at a classification threshold is distinguished from score calibration.
